# Prime Contracts

_How Focus operates on engagements where it holds the prime contract._

On prime engagements, the general [Compliance](../compliance.md) baseline applies to everyone. As the prime, Focus also carries obligations a subcontractor does not: the direct client relationship, subcontractor oversight, and reporting to the contracting officer. This page maps the full lifecycle of a Focus prime contract, from the moment we name someone key personnel in a bid through closeout. The [CDO Engagement Model](cdo-engagement-model.md) carries the operating detail (meeting cadence, escalation, FAQs) that sits underneath it.

We are writing this down now because we are entering a new phase. Focus is winning and running its own prime work (starting with ACF), and the way we staff and stand up a prime decides whether it succeeds. Most of our hard lessons come from moving people onto new work without setting them up to do it well. The framework below is built to stop that from happening again.

## Two principles that run through the whole lifecycle

**Notify early.** The moment a person is named key personnel on a bid, other people's plans depend on it: the program they're on today, the leads who'll have to backfill them, the client who may lose them. We tell those people as early as we can, not when the transition is already on top of us. Early notice is what makes transition planning, backfill, and restaffing possible instead of reactive. This starts in BD, before we've even won.

**Right people in the right seats.** Staffing a prime is the single most important thing we do, and being a Focus employee is not the same as being right for the seat. Every person considered for a key-personnel or lead role, current employees included, gets evaluated against the competencies the role actually requires. Sometimes that means someone we hoped to move into a lead slot doesn't meet the role, and we hire outside instead. We would rather have that conversation before the contract starts than manage the consequences after. (This mirrors the role-scorecard discipline in Claire Hughes Johnson's _Scaling People_: hire and place against the role's defined competencies, not against who is available.)

## Prime Contract Lifecycle

The lifecycle has ten phases. The first begins during capture, before award; the last begins six months before the contract ends. Each names its trigger and its owner. Ownership shifts as the contract matures: the CDO and contract leads stand it up together, and once it's running, the contract leads (the program manager who owns the account) own it through delivery, growth, and wind-down.

### 0. Capture & Key-Personnel Notification (BD)

_Trigger: Focus names key personnel in a bid. Owner: BD, with the affected program leads._

When BD identifies the people it intends to name as key personnel, it checks where those people work today. If they are on a current Nava subcontract, another prime, or a JV, BD notifies that program's lead that the person has been submitted as KP on a pending bid. The point is not to ask permission; it is to get everyone into transition planning early, so the losing program can think through impact, backfill, and restaffing while there is still time to do it well. Track these notifications so they don't live only in one person's head.

### 1. Award & BD-to-Delivery Handoff

_Trigger: notice of award. Owner: BD, handing off to the assigned key personnel._

Winning the contract triggers the prime lifecycle. The first step is a working session where BD walks the assigned key personnel through the market research behind the bid, the proposal, the assumptions made during the bid, and the proposed staffing plan. This is the first real conversation about how the team wants to approach the work and how it wants to operate, and the first chance to surface gaps between what was promised and what's realistic before those gaps become delivery problems.

### 2. Staffing & Recruiting

_Trigger: handoff complete. Owner: practice lead(s) with the Chief Delivery Officer, run as a kickoff meeting._

Assess bench strength against what the contract requires and decide where to hire. Even when the intended person is already a Focus employee, the practice lead meets with the CDO (and whoever else the seat needs) to work the staffing plan as a formal kickoff, not a hallway decision.

- **Evaluate everyone against the role's competencies.** Current employees and outside candidates alike are measured against what the role requires. Someone on a subcontract today whom we hoped to move into a KP or lead slot may, on evaluation, not meet the role. When that happens, we hire outside. Say it plainly and early.
- **Set people up to succeed.** Anyone changing roles gets a job description written for the new role and the support to actually carry it out. Moving a person without this is the mistake we are trying to stop repeating.
- **Start clearances now.** Kick off any required security clearances immediately, given the lead times (federal adjudications routinely run months). Clearance timelines drive start dates more than almost anything else.

### 3. Transition Planning & Internal Pre-Kickoff

_Trigger: seats are filled. Owner: the contract's delivery lead, ideally a program manager._

Focus is moving to a model where a dedicated program manager leads delivery on prime contracts, and we now hire for that role. That delivery lead owns transition planning and runs an internal pre-kickoff that sets up how Focus will work on this contract before we ever meet the client. It covers:

- The transition cadence from now to start date
- Distributing the contract, PWS/SOW, and key clauses to the leads who manage scope day to day
- Risk management and the initial risk register
- System access, and whether we're using government furnished equipment (GFE)
- The deliverables the contract requires in the first 90 days, and a concrete plan to hit them
- Knowledge transfer off current assignments, completed before people start on the new one

### 4. Internal Kickoff & Team Formation

_Trigger: internal pre-kickoff done, the team is assembling. Owner: contract leads and key personnel._

Before Focus sits down with the client, the team comes together as a team. A new team forms, storms, and norms before it performs (Tuckman's model is the shorthand), and we would rather run that deliberately than let it play out live in front of the client. This is an internal kickoff, held ahead of the client kickoff:

- Team building, and explicit agreement on how the team makes decisions (RACI or DACI) and how it works together
- Establishing the delivery operations methodology for this contract
- The AI and tooling conversation, up front: can we use AI on this engagement, does everyone have a cloud license, is everyone comfortable using it

Have these conversations now. They are far cheaper to settle here than to retrofit in month three.

### 5. Client Kickoff

_Trigger: the team is formed. Owner: contract leads._

The client kickoff is a major milestone, and the contract leads run it, not the CDO or CEO. That only works with enough lead time between notice of award and the kickoff for the leads to get through transition and team formation and into a rhythm where they can genuinely prepare. Protect that runway when negotiating the start date; a rushed kickoff is a bad first impression the client remembers.

### 6. First 90-Day Plan

_Trigger: client kickoff done. Owner: delivery lead / program manager with contract leads._

The first 90 days are the most critical stretch of a new contract, and they run on their own plan rather than dropping straight into steady-state cadence. This is when we build the working relationships and map the stakeholder ecosystem: who holds influence, who signs off, who to keep close. It's also where the earliest contract deliverables come due, often at 30 and 60 days. Name those deliverables (building on what was identified in transition planning), own the dates, and work a concrete plan to hit them. How these 90 days go shapes how the client judges Focus for the rest of the contract.

### 7. Steady-State Delivery

_Trigger: the first-90-day plan is delivered and the team is performing. Owner: the program manager, now the account owner._

Most government contracts run on Scrum or SAFe, and in most cases we assimilate into the client's existing methodology, ceremonies, and practices rather than importing our own. As the contract matures, the delivery lead / program manager takes on the account manager role and owns the account: the client relationship, delivery, and the growth of the engagement. The CDO steps back to a lighter oversight cadence, supporting on contracts and negotiations (see the [CDO Engagement Model](cdo-engagement-model.md)).

### 8. Option Years & Recompetes

_Trigger: the option-year and recompete windows. Owner: the program manager who owns the account, with the CDO on contracts._

Option years and recompetes are delivery-led growth, and they are the account-owning program manager's job, not something that gets handed off. This is part of how a PM's success is measured. Escalate to practice or executive leadership as needed, but the PM drives it:

- **Option years: at least six months out.** Start checking whether the contract is on track, whether the client intends to exercise, and whether anything needs attention before the decision point.
- **Recompetes: a full year out.** A recompete needs a strategy a year in advance. ACF is a live example: we're in the second option year and the contract may recompete within a year, so the work starts now, client intimacy, on-site visits, understanding agency priorities and strategy, so we can position for a sole-source continuation or, if it's competed, help shape the solicitation.

### 9. Closeout & Continuity

_Trigger: roughly six months before the contract ends or changes. Owner: the program manager, with the CDO on the client-facing exit._

Whether a contract ends on schedule, isn't recompeted, or we lose a recompete, give it a six-month runway:

- **Continuity planning for our people.** Identify where staff can move next, well before the last week.
- **What the client needs to keep running.** The training, SOPs, and documentation that let the client (or an incoming contractor) operate after we leave.
- **Preserve the relationship.** Manage the exit to protect the relationship regardless of why the contract is ending. We can come back to this client.
- **Knowledge transfer to whoever comes next.** If another company is taking over, transfer knowledge to them the way we would want an incumbent to do for us, and give them a real onboarding.

## Quarterly Program Review

Prime engagements get their own quarterly review, separate from the subcontract portfolio review. Same shape: program status, staffing, risks (technical, contractual, client, and political), and opportunities, with the addition of subcontractor performance and prime-side reporting obligations.
