# Job Aid 03: Conducting Exit Interviews

_A reusable interview guide and notes template for learning from a departing team member's experience at Focus._

An exit interview helps Focus understand what supported someone’s success, what made their work harder, and what influenced their decision to leave. Use the conversation to identify practices worth preserving and improvements in management, onboarding, team relationships, and delivery operations.

This guide works across roles, practices, and client engagements. Plan for **30 minutes**, with up to **45 minutes** if the departing person wants more time. Ask the core questions first and use the optional prompts selectively. For a planned contract end or other departure the person did not choose, adapt the departure questions to their experience of the transition.

Jump to the [30-minute interview](#the-30-minute-interview), [optional prompts](#optional-prompts-for-a-longer-interview), [notes template](#copyable-interview-notes-template), or [action tracker](#copyable-action-tracker).

## Before the Interview

- **Choose an interviewer who can receive candid feedback.** Where possible, offer someone outside the direct reporting relationship. If the feedback concerns the interviewer, offer another appropriate Focus leader or People Operations contact.
- **Explain the purpose in advance.** Invite participation, allow the person to skip questions, and offer written feedback or a shorter follow-up if they prefer. Keep equipment return, final administrative tasks, and handover logistics in a separate conversation.
- **Confirm how feedback will be handled.** Identify who needs access to the notes and who will receive a summary. Do not promise complete confidentiality or anonymity. Explain that some concerns may need to be shared with the people responsible for addressing them.
- **Prepare without forming a verdict.** Review the role and dates, then separate what you know from assumptions about the departure. Do not bring a list of accusations for the person to confirm.
- **Use a private place for notes.** Confirm the appropriate restricted location with People Operations. This handbook contains the blank template only; completed notes, recordings, transcripts, and identifying examples belong outside the public handbook and its repository. If recording or using transcription, explain the tool and intended access and obtain explicit agreement before starting.

## Opening Script

Allow about two minutes. Replace the bracketed text with the actual sharing arrangements before the conversation.

> “Thank you for taking the time to talk. I want to understand your experience at Focus, what we should keep doing, and what we should improve. You can skip any question or choose to share feedback in writing.
>
> “I’ll take notes so I can represent your feedback accurately. The notes will be available to [people or roles who need access], and I plan to share [type of summary] with [recipients]. I can’t promise complete confidentiality: if you raise something that needs attention, I may need to involve the appropriate people. I’ll explain the next steps as clearly as I can.
>
> “I’m here to listen and understand. Before we start, is there anything you would like to know about how this conversation or your feedback will be handled?”

## The 30 Minute Interview

The timings include the opening and total 30 minutes. Ask each numbered question, then choose a follow-up where it will clarify something important. **Protect the final nine minutes for the departure decision and closing.** If an earlier issue needs more time, offer a follow-up rather than silently dropping those topics.

### 1. Overall Experience · 3 Minutes

**How would you describe your experience at Focus overall?**

Possible follow-ups:

- What are you most proud of, or what did you enjoy most?
- What was most disappointing or harder than it needed to be?

Capture both strengths and difficulties. Let the person choose their own words before suggesting descriptions of the culture.

### 2. Joining Focus and Understanding the Role · 4 Minutes

**How did the role and your day-to-day work compare with what you expected when you joined?**

Possible follow-ups:

- What helped you get started, and what was missing from onboarding?
- How clear were success expectations for you and for the team?
- When did you feel part of the team? What helped or delayed that?

Distinguish understanding an individual assignment from understanding the team’s goals, priorities, and definition of success.

### 3. Manager and Leadership Support · 4 Minutes

**What was it like working with your manager, and where did you need more support?**

Possible follow-ups:

- What did your manager do that helped you succeed?
- What feedback, coaching, or conversation would have helped but did not happen?
- What could Focus leadership, including me, have done differently?

If the person had both a People Manager and a day-to-day lead, distinguish those relationships. Positive feedback about one kind of support does not answer every question about management or development.

### 4. Team Relationships and Delivery · 4 Minutes

**How did the team work together, and where did collaboration become difficult?**

Possible follow-ups:

- Can you describe one decision or handoff that worked well or broke down?
- How clear was who could decide, who needed to contribute, and how priorities changed?
- How did working relationships affect your ability to do good work?

Ask about relevant roles and disciplines without assuming a particular team structure. If a specific relationship matters, ask: “What was it like working with [person]? What worked, and what was difficult?”

### 5. Speaking Up and Respect · 4 Minutes

**How comfortable did you feel disagreeing, asking for help, or raising a concern?**

Possible follow-ups:

- Can you share an example of how people responded?
- Were there times you decided not to raise something? What influenced that decision?
- How were mistakes handled, and did you feel treated with respect?

Feeling respected, feeling supported, and feeling safe to disagree are related but distinct. Do not infer that all three were present from an answer about only one.

### 6. Decision to Leave · 5 Minutes

**What factors influenced your decision to leave, and when did you begin considering it?**

Possible follow-ups:

- Was there a turning point, or did the decision develop over time?
- What mattered most in choosing your next step?
- Was there anything Focus could realistically have changed that might have influenced your decision?

Allow for several reasons, including positive opportunities elsewhere. Do not assume an improvement suggestion is a reason for leaving unless the person makes that connection. If the departure was not their choice, ask instead: “How have you experienced this transition, and what could Focus have handled better?”

### 7. Advice and Closing · 4 Minutes

**What should Focus preserve or change for the next person in a similar role?**

Then ask: **“Is there anything I haven’t asked that you want leadership to understand?”**

Briefly reflect the main points back: “I heard [strength], [concern], and [suggested change]. What have I missed or misunderstood?” Confirm any agreed follow-up and thank the person. Do not promise an outcome you cannot deliver.

## Optional Prompts for a Longer Interview

Use the extra time to clarify the most relevant topics. This is a question bank, not an additional checklist to finish.

| Topic | Prompts |
| --- | --- |
| Role and use of skills | Were you able to use your strengths? What work did you expect to do but rarely had time or opportunity to do? |
| Development and feedback | What helped you grow? What feedback or practice would have helped you improve? How clear were future opportunities? |
| Onboarding and access | What was easy or difficult about equipment, access, documentation, introductions, and learning the client context? |
| Workload and flexibility | How manageable were the workload and competing priorities? How did working hours, flexibility, and time away work in practice? |
| Compensation and benefits | Did compensation, benefits, or progression influence your experience or decision? What would you like leadership to understand? |
| Connection to Focus | How connected did you feel to Focus beyond your immediate project? What helped you learn from people elsewhere in the company? |
| Client and contract environment | How did client expectations, approvals, dependencies, or staffing arrangements affect the work? What was within Focus’s control to improve? |
| Cross-functional work | How did information move between disciplines? Where did another perspective or a clearer handoff change the result? |
| Specific working relationships | What worked well with this person? What was difficult? What happened, and how did it affect you or the work? |

## How to Listen and Follow Up

Aim to spend most of the conversation listening. After a useful response, ask one neutral follow-up: **“Can you give me an example?”**, **“What effect did that have?”**, or **“What would have helped?”** Allow a pause before moving on.

- **Reflect before explaining.** Check your understanding of the person’s experience. Save proposed solutions and explanations of leadership decisions for after you have heard the full account.
- **Avoid leading questions.** Replace “Was your manager unsupportive?” with “Where did you need more support?” Replace “Did the team’s conflict make you leave?” with “What factors influenced your decision?”
- **Separate accounts.** Record what the person experienced, what they observed in others, and your own interpretation distinctly. An interviewer’s explanation or a brief “yes” to it is not the same as an independently described experience.
- **Respect what remains unknown.** Mark questions as answered, partially answered, not asked, or declined. Do not fill gaps with assumptions or treat a declined answer as confirmation of a concern.
- **Respond appropriately to serious concerns.** If the person raises conduct, safety, security, or other reportable concerns, explain and follow the applicable [Conduct & Ethics](../../workplace.md) or [Incident Response](../incident-response.md) path. Do not wait for a routine interview summary or require them to confront the person involved first.

## Assess the Feedback and Assign Actions

After the conversation, prepare a concise summary for the agreed recipients. Keep identifiable personnel details in the appropriate restricted record. Removing a name may not make a story anonymous, especially on a small team.

Summarize:

1. **What worked:** practices and support worth preserving.
2. **What made work harder:** concrete examples and their reported impact.
3. **Departure factors:** only the reasons the person actually stated; identify what remains unknown.
4. **Assessment:** possible individual, management, or delivery-system issues and what needs further checking. One interview can justify a practical improvement without proving a company-wide pattern or an individual conduct finding.
5. **Actions:** a small number of concrete changes with an accountable owner, target date, and a way to check whether the change helped.

Use [Job Aid 01](coaching-performance-systemic-issues.md) when distinguishing coaching, performance, and systemic concerns. Use [Job Aid 02](giving-effective-feedback.md) when preparing an appropriate feedback conversation. Serious concerns continue through the existing reporting process.

### Suggested Follow-Through

- **Within two business days:** complete the notes, identify unanswered questions, and confirm any follow-up promised to the departing person. Route urgent concerns when raised, without waiting for this step.
- **Within two weeks:** review the summary with the appropriate leadership or People Operations contacts and assign owners and dates to accepted actions. Distinguish proposed changes from decisions already made.
- **At 30 and 60 days:** check whether actions changed the experience or the work. Ask current team members or new hires for concrete examples; completed meetings and updated documents alone do not establish improvement.

Use these as planning targets, adapting to the situation and any applicable process. When sharing team-level improvements, explain what is changing without exposing private interview details.
