# Job Aid 02: Giving Effective Feedback

_Why feedback matters and how managers, leads, and teammates can give useful feedback to one another._

Feedback gives people information they need to succeed: what is working, what needs to change, and how their actions affect others. When a concern stays unspoken or circulates among colleagues, the person involved has little opportunity to understand it or respond.

Feedback flows in every direction: between teammates, from leads to team members, and from team members to leads and managers. You do not need a management title or reporting relationship to share an observation, explain its impact, or ask for a change in how you work together.

This job aid applies principles from Claire Hughes Johnson's [*Scaling People*](https://press.stripe.com/purchase-scaling-people) and *Crucial Conversations* to Focus delivery situations. The examples and suggested cadence below are practical applications for our teams.

## Why Giving Feedback Is Part of Working Together

Each of us sees effects of the work that others may miss: the downstream effect of a late handoff, the uncertainty created by a missing update, or the value of a well-timed escalation. Sharing those observations helps people connect their work to team outcomes, regardless of role or seniority.

- **Make expectations visible.** Refer to shared agreements and explain what you need to work well together. Managers and leads should also clarify expectations for a person's role and level. If an expectation has not been discussed, establish it together or involve the person responsible for setting it.
- **Give people time to adjust.** Raise concerns while the details are fresh and there is an opportunity to practice a different approach. Waiting for a review or the next retro can allow the same issue to repeat.
- **Reinforce what works.** Specific positive feedback tells people which behaviors to repeat and helps the team learn from good delivery practices.
- **Improve shared understanding.** Either person may learn about unclear priorities, missing support, or a decision that needs to be revisited.

> A concern discussed with others still needs an appropriate conversation with the person involved. Talking about someone does not give them feedback.

This supports Focus's existing approach of [active coaching and clear expectations](../../performance.md). Everyone can give feedback; formal performance assessment and corrective action remain with the responsible managers and appropriate Focus leadership/People Operations. [Job Aid 01](coaching-performance-systemic-issues.md) helps managers and leads distinguish coaching, performance, and systemic concerns as they learn more.

## Principles from Scaling People

Hughes Johnson emphasizes managerial self-awareness, curiosity when coaching, and the willingness to surface difficult topics. She describes feedback as a partnership in which the manager offers observations and explores what is happening, rather than assuming they already have the answer. See her [discussion of these principles](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/mckinsey-on-books/author-talks-scale-your-people-not-just-your-company).

Although Hughes Johnson discusses these principles in a management context, the same curiosity and self-awareness are useful in peer feedback and feedback to a lead or manager. Apply that approach by:

- **Checking your own contribution.** Was the shared agreement clear? Did your own actions or requests contribute to the problem? Are you reacting to a work-style preference or a meaningful delivery gap?
- **Bringing an observation and a question.** Be specific about what you saw, then investigate the explanation together.
- **Naming the concern respectfully.** If a recurring issue matters to the work or relationship, make it discussable. Avoid hints that require the other person to guess what you mean.

## Prepare for a Crucial Conversation

**Start with Heart:** Identify the outcome you want for the person, the team, and your working relationship. Check whether frustration has shifted your goal toward winning an argument or proving someone wrong. Explain your constructive purpose when you open the conversation. [Crucial Learning: Start with Heart](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-start-with-heart/).

**Make It Safe:** Show respect for the person and concern for shared goals. If the conversation becomes defensive or shuts down, pause to understand what feels threatening and clarify your intent. Give them room to disagree and correct your interpretation while keeping the expectation clear. [Crucial Learning: Make It Safe](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-make-it-safe/).

For sensitive individual feedback, choose a private conversation with enough time to listen. Prepare one or two concrete examples and the impact you can substantiate. Distinguish what you observed directly from what someone else reported, and avoid presenting secondhand concerns as established facts.

## Use STATE to Open the Dialogue

The STATE framework helps you explain your perspective and invite the other person's. The last two skills describe how to speak throughout the conversation. [Crucial Learning's explanation of STATE](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/what-to-say-when-people-break-boundaries/).

| Skill | How to apply it |
| --- | --- |
| **Share your facts** | Describe a specific event or behavior that could be observed. Avoid labels such as “unreliable” or “not committed.” |
| **Tell your story** | Explain the concern or interpretation the facts raise for you. Distinguish an actual impact from a risk you anticipate. |
| **Ask for others' paths** | Invite their account of what happened. Listen before choosing a response. |
| **Talk tentatively** | Treat your explanation of the cause as something to examine. You can be certain about an agreed expectation while remaining curious about why it was missed. |
| **Encourage testing** | Explicitly welcome corrections, missing context, and a different interpretation. Change your view when the evidence warrants it. |

### Example: Repeated Follow-Ups

> “I'd like us to make handoffs easier to plan. On the last two deliverables, the agreed status update wasn't posted, and I asked twice before learning the next steps. That left me unable to confirm the handoff with the team. I'm concerned our current approach isn't making progress and blockers visible enough. What was happening from your perspective? Is there context I'm missing?”

Pause and listen. If the update expectation was unclear, clarify the agreement together. If competing assignments prevented progress, involve the lead who can resolve the priorities. If an agreed update was missed, discuss what would make the handoff work more reliably.

Then agree on a concrete next step, for example:

> “Could we both post progress, the next step, and any blocker in the agreed channel before our check-in for the next two weeks? I'll ask our lead to clarify which assignment takes priority today. Let's check together next week to see whether handoffs are clearer.”

### Example: Feedback to a Lead or Manager

> “In the last two planning meetings, a new priority was added without identifying what should move out. I left unsure which commitment to keep, and work on the original priority stalled. Could we make the tradeoff explicit when priorities change? Is there context about those decisions that would help me understand them?”

If you are receiving feedback from someone you lead, listen, acknowledge the impact, and explain what you will change or clarify. Your response helps determine whether they will feel able to raise the next concern.

### Example: Reinforcing Good Practice

> “You flagged the dependency risk before planning and brought two options. That gave us time to adjust the commitment with the client. Please keep surfacing risks that early; it helps the team make decisions while we still have options.”

## Common Scenarios

| Situation | Useful response |
| --- | --- |
| A concern appears in a retro | Use the retro to understand the team pattern. Follow up privately on individual behavior; do not turn the group discussion into an individual performance review. |
| You are waiting for the person to notice the problem | State the observation, impact, and expectation directly. Seniority does not make unspoken expectations clear. |
| The person disagrees or becomes upset | Listen for missing facts or a misunderstanding. A reaction alone does not establish a performance issue. If needed, agree on a short pause and a specific time to resume. |
| The same gap continues after feedback | Revisit the agreement and what remains unresolved. If you cannot resolve it together, bring concrete examples and the steps already tried to the appropriate lead or People Manager. Managers and leads can use Job Aid 01 to assess the concern; formal performance action requires the appropriate Focus leadership/People Operations involvement. |
| Someone gives you difficult feedback | Ask for an example, check your understanding, and explain what you will do with the input. Follow up on your own commitments. |

For issues covered by Focus's [Conduct & Ethics](../../workplace.md) or [Incident Response](../incident-response.md) procedures, use the applicable reporting path; this job aid does not add a requirement to confront someone first.

## End with Action and Follow Through

*Crucial Conversations* emphasizes turning dialogue into specific commitments: identify an owner, the action, a deadline, and a follow-up. A conversation can feel productive while leaving those decisions unresolved. [Al Switzler on moving to action](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/improve-results-by-moving-to-action/).

Before closing, confirm:

- The behavior to continue or change and what success will look like.
- The support or changes each person can provide, and anything that needs a lead's or manager's decision.
- Who is responsible for each action and when it will happen.
- When you will review progress together.

When a recap would help, confirm the agreement privately with the other person, including both perspectives and each person's commitments. Managers should use the appropriate management record when formal documentation is needed. Recognize improvement at follow-up and address any remaining gap explicitly.

### Suggested Practice Before the Next Retro

Use the next 30 days as a learning window, adjusting timing to the situation:

1. **Early in the window:** Choose one meaningful concern, gather examples, and have the conversation. Also identify a helpful behavior to reinforce.
2. **During the window:** Check progress at an agreed check-in with the other person. Complete your own commitments and adjust the approach if new information changes your understanding.
3. **At the next retro:** Discuss team-level actions, what changed, and what remains blocked. Keep individual feedback and personnel details in the appropriate private conversations.

The next retro is a checkpoint. Give feedback when it can help, rather than saving it for that meeting.
