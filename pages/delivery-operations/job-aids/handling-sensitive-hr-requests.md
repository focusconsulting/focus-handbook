# Job Aid 04: Handling Sensitive HR Requests (Leave, Accommodations, and Protected Situations)

_How to recognize legally sensitive requests, route them to People Operations within 24 hours, and document them correctly — so Focus stays compliant and the team member is supported._

Some requests that reach a manager carry legal obligations for Focus the moment they are made. A team member mentioning a pregnancy, a surgery, a family member's illness, a disability, a religious observance, or military service is not a casual conversation — it can trigger federal or state protections around **leave, accommodation, and non-retaliation**.

The manager's job in these moments is **not** to approve, deny, evaluate, or investigate the request. The manager's job is to **recognize it, respond supportively, route it to People Operations within 24 hours, and document it in writing.** People Operations — **Meg Greenberg** — determines eligibility, obligations, and next steps, involving legal counsel where needed.

> **This is process guidance, not legal advice, and it does not create or change any Focus policy or benefit.** When in doubt about whether something qualifies, escalate it. Over-escalating a request is safe. Sitting on one is not.

Jump to [what counts](#requests-that-must-be-escalated), the [24-hour SLA](#the-sla-24-hours-to-meg-greenberg), the [manager process](#the-manager-process), [what not to do](#what-managers-must-not-do), or the [documentation templates](#documentation-templates).

## Why This Matters

Under laws like the **FMLA**, the **ADA**, the **Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA)**, Title VII, USERRA, and various state leave and paid-family-leave statutes, an employee does **not** have to use the right words to trigger Focus's obligations. There are **no magic words.** A team member does not need to say "FMLA," "ADA," or "accommodation." Saying *"I'm having a baby in the spring,"* *"my chemo starts next month,"* *"my back injury makes it hard to sit all day,"* or *"I can't work Saturdays for religious reasons"* is enough to start the clock.

That means the manager who hears the request first is the front line of compliance. Missing, delaying, or mishandling one of these requests can expose Focus to legal liability and, more importantly, fails a team member at a vulnerable moment.

## Requests That Must Be Escalated

Treat any request that touches the categories below as sensitive and route it to Meg Greenberg. This list is illustrative, not exhaustive — **when a request resembles these, escalate it.**

| Category | What it can look like from a team member | Underlying protection (managers do **not** determine this) |
|---|---|---|
| **Parental / family leave** | "I'm pregnant," "we're adopting," "my partner is due in March," "I need to plan paternity leave." | FMLA, state paid family leave, Focus parental-leave policy |
| **Medical / personal health leave** | "I need surgery," "I'll be out for treatment," "my doctor is putting me on leave," a pattern of health-related absences. | FMLA, state medical leave, ADA |
| **Family caregiving leave** | "My parent/child/spouse is seriously ill and needs care." | FMLA, state paid family & medical leave |
| **FMLA specifically** | Any mention of a serious health condition — their own or a covered family member's — or continuous/intermittent time off for one. | FMLA (and state equivalents) |
| **Disability accommodation** | "I need a different setup / schedule / tool / remote arrangement because of a health condition," physical or mental. | ADA / state disability law |
| **Pregnancy accommodation** | "I need lighter duties / more breaks / to avoid X while pregnant or recovering," or lactation/pumping needs. | PWFA, PUMP Act, state law |
| **Religious accommodation** | "I can't work on the Sabbath / a holy day," dress or grooming needs, prayer breaks. | Title VII / state law |
| **Mental health** | "I'm dealing with anxiety/depression and need adjustments or time off." | ADA (treated like any other health condition) |
| **Workplace injury** | "I got hurt on the job / doing work." | Workers' compensation |
| **Military service** | "I'm being deployed / have drill / Guard obligations." | USERRA, military leave laws |
| **Other protected time off** | Jury duty, bereavement, voting, domestic-violence/victim leave, or anything the team member frames as a legal right. | Various federal/state statutes |

### Escalate Immediately — Not on the 24-Hour Clock

A separate set of situations should go to Meg Greenberg (and, where relevant, appropriate leadership) **right away, not at the end of a 24-hour window**, because the risk is time-sensitive:

- A complaint of **harassment, discrimination, or retaliation** (including on the basis of any protected characteristic).
- A **threat of harm, violence, or a safety emergency.**
- A report that someone is **being treated worse because they asked for leave or an accommodation** (retaliation).

These are outside the scope of routine leave/accommodation intake and follow Focus's escalation and reporting channels. If a request mixes categories — for example, a leave request *and* a complaint of unfair treatment — escalate on the faster track.

## The SLA: 24 Hours to Meg Greenberg

**Every sensitive request in the table above must reach Meg Greenberg, in writing, within 24 hours of the manager becoming aware of it** — including requests made verbally, in passing, in a one-on-one, over chat, or that the manager merely infers from what a team member said.

| Step | Owner | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledge the request to the team member | Manager | Same day / same conversation |
| Written summary routed to Meg Greenberg (People Ops) | Manager | **Within 24 hours** |
| People Ops acknowledges and takes ownership of next steps | Meg Greenberg | Within 1 business day of receipt |
| Formal next steps (eligibility, paperwork, interactive process) begin | People Ops | Per legal/policy requirements, tracked by People Ops |

The 24-hour SLA is a floor, not a target to fill. **Escalate the same day whenever you can.** The clock starts when the manager first learns of the request — not when the team member formally "submits" anything, and not when it is convenient.

## The Manager Process

When a sensitive request comes in, work through these five steps.

**1. Recognize.** If the request touches leave, a health or family situation, a disability, pregnancy, religion, military service, or an on-the-job injury, treat it as sensitive — even if the team member was casual or used no legal terms.

**2. Respond supportively and neutrally.** Thank them for letting you know. Reassure them that you'll connect them with People Operations, who handle these directly and confidentially. Do not evaluate, promise, or push back.

> "Thanks for telling me — I want to make sure you're supported and that we handle this the right way. Meg Greenberg on our People Operations team manages these, and I'll loop her in today so she can walk you through the details and any options. Nothing you need to figure out alone."

**3. Capture it in writing.** Get the basics down: who, what they're requesting, and any timing they mentioned. Ask the team member to confirm their request in a short email (template below) copying you and Meg — or, if they'd rather not write it themselves, document what they told you and send it on their behalf, copying them so they can confirm it's accurate. **Do not ask for medical details or diagnoses** (see below).

**4. Route to Meg Greenberg within 24 hours.** Send the written summary to Meg, copying the team member where appropriate. This starts People Operations' formal handling.

**5. Hand off and support.** People Operations owns eligibility, paperwork, the interactive/accommodation process, and any determinations. The manager's ongoing role is to keep the team member supported, keep the information confidential, and follow People Ops' guidance — not to manage the case.

## What Managers Must NOT Do

These are the most common ways a well-meaning manager creates legal exposure. Avoid all of them.

- **Do not ask for a diagnosis or medical details.** You may know someone needs leave or an adjustment; you do not need — and should not ask — *what's wrong with them*. Let People Ops request any documentation through the proper channel.
- **Do not decide eligibility or approve/deny anything.** Not FMLA eligibility, not whether an accommodation is "reasonable," not leave duration. That is People Operations' and, where needed, legal counsel's call.
- **Do not delay, discourage, or talk them out of it.** No "can this wait until after the sprint?", "are you sure you need that?", or "we're really slammed right now." Discouraging a protected request can itself be unlawful interference.
- **Do not make promises.** Don't promise approval, a specific length of leave, that their exact role will be held, or how pay will work. People Ops communicates the actual terms.
- **Do not treat the person differently afterward.** No changes to assignments, reviews, opportunities, or tone because they asked. That risks a retaliation claim.
- **Do not share the information.** Keep it strictly between you, the team member, and People Ops on a need-to-know basis. Never discuss it in team channels, standups, or with peers.
- **Do not store medical or leave details in team tools.** Keep it out of Slack, project trackers, retro docs, and shared drives. Written records go to People Operations.
- **Do not ignore an informal or verbal request.** "I might need some time off when the baby comes" still starts the clock. Escalate it.

## Documentation Templates

Focus needs a written record of every sensitive request. The goal is a simple, dated, factual note — **not** medical detail. Use whichever template fits how the request came in.

### Template A — Team Member Confirms Their Own Request

Share this with the team member and ask them to send it to you and Meg. Keep it light; they should not feel they need to disclose anything medical.

> **To:** Meg Greenberg (People Operations); [Manager]
> **Subject:** Request — [leave / accommodation / other]
>
> Hi Meg,
>
> I'd like to request [brief description of the request — e.g., "parental leave around my due date in March," "a schedule adjustment for a medical reason," "leave to care for a family member"].
>
> The approximate timing is [dates / start date / "still being determined"]. [Manager] is copied so they're aware. I'd appreciate guidance on next steps and any paperwork.
>
> Thanks,
> [Team member]

### Template B — Manager Documents a Verbal Request

Use this when the request came up in conversation and the manager is memorializing it. Copy the team member so they can confirm accuracy.

> **To:** Meg Greenberg (People Operations)
> **Cc:** [Team member]
> **Subject:** Sensitive request routed to People Ops — [team member name]
>
> Hi Meg,
>
> Flagging a request for People Operations to take from here. On [date], [team member] let me know that [factual summary of what they requested — e.g., "they are expecting a child this spring and want to plan parental leave," or "they need a workstation adjustment for a health reason"]. Approximate timing: [dates / "TBD"].
>
> I let them know you'd follow up on next steps and that this stays confidential. I did not ask for and am not including any medical detail. [Team member] is copied to confirm I've captured this correctly.
>
> Thanks,
> [Manager]

### What to Record

- **Date** the manager learned of the request.
- **Who** made it.
- **What** was requested, in the team member's own framing.
- **Timing** mentioned, if any.
- **That it was routed to People Operations**, and when.

Deliberately leave out: diagnoses, medical specifics, opinions on whether it's justified, or any evaluation. Facts and dates only.

## Quick Reference

| If a team member... | The manager should... |
|---|---|
| Mentions a pregnancy, birth, or adoption | Escalate to Meg within 24 hours; do not wait for a "formal" request. |
| Says they or a family member has a serious health condition | Escalate within 24 hours; do not ask for the diagnosis. |
| Asks for a change to how/when/where they work for a health, pregnancy, or religious reason | Escalate within 24 hours as a possible accommodation; do not approve or deny it. |
| Reports a work-related injury | Escalate within 24 hours (workers' comp) and follow any safety-reporting steps. |
| Reports harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or a safety threat | Escalate **immediately**, not on the 24-hour clock. |
| Makes a request casually or without legal terms | Treat it as real; there are no magic words. Escalate. |
| Asks the manager to decide or keep it "just between us" | Explain that People Operations handles these and must be looped in, kept confidential and need-to-know. |
